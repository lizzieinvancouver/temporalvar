\begin{abstract}
% Megan says high propagule production -- is stuff coming in from outside; I think this is true for larvae....
Invasive species often exhibit high propagule production (is there a friendlier general term?) under many different environmental conditions. Plant invaders often germinate at high rates across a suite of different temperature regimes, while native species appear to require highly specific cues and, even then, germinate at low rates. While this reality may explain why invasive species are so successful, it leaves open a massive question of what the hell the native species are doing. Here, we argue that answering this question requires reframing how we think about germination, survival and species extirpation alongside reframing the relevant timescales of environmental variation that we consider. We outline how the strategy of specifically timed, forever-low germination may be favored under certain climatic conditions. While such conditions have been uncommon in the short snapshot of ecological research they are more common over the timescales of species range shifts. % I don't like this timescale, but am struggling to think of the right words ... species lifespans is too long, and paleoclimate is too much of a 'field' not a mechanistic period. 
We show how experiments and new analyses could begin to test this theory, with implications for restoration management. 
\end{abstract}

\emph{Keywords}: propagule pressure, seedbanks, bet-hedging
